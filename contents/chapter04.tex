\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the MeanFlow residual of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} against its two reference heads --- the EDM diffusion baseline it sets out to accelerate and the FlowCast straight-line flow-matching baseline it generalises. Section~\ref{sec:exp-protocol} defines the experimental protocol and the matched-sample-budget convention. Section~\ref{sec:exp-metrics} defines the evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:exp-main} reports the headline comparison on the 2022 validation year. Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} reports the inference-cost (wall-clock and NFE) ablation, where MeanFlow's one-to-two-evaluation operating point is the central result. Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip} drills into precipitation skill by threshold and neighbourhood size; Section~\ref{sec:exp-rollout} reports autoregressive rollout error growth. Sections~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} and~\ref{sec:exp-qpw} report the \texttt{log1p}-encoding and precipitation-weight ablations. Section~\ref{sec:exp-legacy} places the heads against a legacy old-StormCast baseline. Section~\ref{sec:exp-qual} presents qualitative case studies, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-discussion} concludes with a discussion; the failure modes observed in development are catalogued alongside the limitations in Section~\ref{sec:concl-limitations}.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

\paragraph{Validation split.} All reported metrics are computed on the cleaned 2022 full-year RWRF validation store (8{,}759 hourly samples; Section~\ref{sec:method-data}). This contrasts with the stale \texttt{valid\_dates} shipped in \texttt{stormcast/config/inference/stormcast.yaml}, which we override explicitly in every run.

\paragraph{Matched training-sample budget.} The fair-comparison unit is \emph{training samples seen}, not optimiser steps, because the residual heads use different global batch sizes (EDM at batch 64, FlowCast at batch 96, MeanFlow at batch 112), which makes the step-to-sample conversion head-specific. We evaluate each head at the saved checkpoint nearest the $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-training-sample anchor; the MeanFlow checkpoint sits essentially on it. Holding training samples fixed means any quality difference reflects the generative trajectory and not a training-compute advantage.

\paragraph{Single-snapshot noise.} Each validation point is a single snapshot. Adjacent validation logs drift by $10$--$30\,\%$, so differences below $\approx 5\,\%$ on a single metric should not be over-interpreted; we call out only gaps that are large relative to this noise floor.

\paragraph{NFE budgets and samplers.} The EDM baseline samples with its native 18-step Heun schedule (two model evaluations per step except the last, $2N - 1 = 35$ NFE per hourly step). FlowCast samples with $K$ explicit Euler steps, $K$ NFE; we use $K = 10$ in the headline scoreboard, the point at which the straight-line ODE is essentially converged (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}), and report the $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ cost ladder in the timing ablation. MeanFlow samples with $K$ average-velocity segments (Eq.~\eqref{eq:meanflow-sampler}), also exactly $K$ NFE; its headline operating points are $K \in \{1, 2\}$. The scoreboard harness loads the two flow students from their online checkpoints in the same way, so the FlowCast-versus-MeanFlow comparison is symmetric end to end (the EMA shadow is used by the in-training validation sampler; Section~\ref{sec:method-flowcast}).

\paragraph{Rollouts and ensembling.} The scoreboard metrics are computed from autoregressive rollouts driven by \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py}, which denormalises every output (undoing standardisation and the \texttt{log1p} encoding) so that \texttt{qpepre} is scored in mm\,h$^{-1}$ regardless of the on-disk encoding. Following the StormCast ensemble design \citep{pathak2024stormcast}, CRPS uses a \textbf{five-member} per-hour ensemble of independent noise seeds propagated autoregressively. Deterministic and categorical scores use the ensemble mean. Ten initial times are spaced evenly across the validation year, each rolled out autoregressively over a \textbf{six-hour} horizon ($+1\,\text{h}\,\ldots\,+6\,\text{h}$), matching StormCast's headline 1--6\,h skill window.

\paragraph{Hardware.} Training: multi-GPU single node. Evaluation: single GPU. The ``Time/Seq.'' column in the scoreboards is the wall-clock cost of generating one five-member six-hour rollout sequence on the evaluation GPU; the dedicated inference-timing ablation of Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} reports the operationally relevant cost at a 50-member, 24-hour configuration on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp-metrics}

All metrics are computed on denormalised fields with physical units (K, m\,s$^{-1}$, mm\,h$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{RMSE and MAE.} For channel $k$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|\,HW}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat{M}^{(k)}_{t,i,j} - M^{(k)}_{t,i,j}\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
with MAE defined analogously.

\paragraph{CRPS.} The continuous ranked probability score evaluates the full predictive distribution of the small per-hour ensemble against the observation; lower is better. It rewards a forecast that is both sharp and calibrated, and is our primary probabilistic score.

\paragraph{Categorical precipitation scores.} For threshold $\tau$ (mm\,h$^{-1}$), classify each pixel as wet ($\geq \tau$) or dry. With hits $H$, misses $M$, false alarms $F$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CSI}(\tau) = \frac{H}{H + M + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) = \frac{F}{H + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{HSS}(\tau) = \frac{2(H\,\mathrm{CN} - M\,F)}{(H+M)(M+\mathrm{CN}) + (H+F)(F+\mathrm{CN})},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CN}$ is the correct-negative count. We report $\tau \in \{0.1, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 16.0\}$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$; ``-M'' suffixes denote the mean over thresholds and ``-P16'' the $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ heavy-rain point. CSI and HSS reward correct detection (higher is better); FAR penalises over-prediction (lower is better). To relax the single-pixel double-penalty, CSI/POD/FAR/HSS are scored with \emph{neighbourhood-max} pooling: an event counts as forecast if any pixel within an $n \times n$ window exceeds $\tau$. We evaluate at the four StormCast pooling windows \citep{pathak2024stormcast} on the $\approx 2$\,km RWRF grid --- $3$\,km (grid scale, $1\times1$\,px), $15$\,km ($7\times7$\,px), $27$\,km ($13\times13$\,px) and $45$\,km ($23\times23$\,px) --- and take the $15$\,km window as the headline.

\paragraph{Fractions Skill Score.} With neighbourhood window $n$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FSS}(\tau, n) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j} - p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2}{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j})^2 + \sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2},
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the \emph{fraction} of $\tau$-exceeding pixels in the $n \times n$ box centred at $(i,j)$ \citep{roberts2008fss}. FSS mitigates the double-penalty problem and is reported at the same four pooling windows; the heavy-rain point $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ is denoted FSS-P16.

\paragraph{Rollout metrics.} Per-channel RMSE is additionally reported as a function of lead time $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}$\,h to expose autoregressive error growth.

\paragraph{Read.} At $K = 10$ FlowCast runs in \SI{3.07}{\second} per five-member sequence against \SI{11.23}{\second} for the $35$-NFE EDM Heun sampler --- a \textbf{3.7$\times$ speedup} --- and at that budget matches or beats the diffusion baseline on most columns: CRPS is statistically indistinguishable ($0.5518$ vs $0.5537$) and FlowCast leads on \texttt{u10} ($-5\,\%$), \texttt{v10} ($-12\,\%$), and \texttt{qpepre} ($-7\,\%$) RMSE, conceding only \texttt{t2m} RMSE ($0.8498$ vs $0.8023$) and the headline-window categorical hits (CSI-M $0.2622$ vs $0.2504$, HSS-M $0.2926$ vs $0.2889$). FAR is within noise at $15$\,km but FlowCast is the lower-false-alarm head at the $3$, $27$, and $45$\,km windows (Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip}); pushing it beyond $K = 10$ buys only wall-clock, the straight-line ODE being essentially converged by $\approx 10$ steps (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}). MeanFlow compresses the cost axis by another large factor --- \SI{1.10}{\second} at $K = 2$ ($\approx 10.2\times$ cheaper than diffusion, $2.8\times$ cheaper than FlowCast) and \SI{0.91}{\second} at $K = 1$ --- and at $K = 2$ this comes with \emph{no} quality penalty: it posts the best CRPS in the table ($0.5207$, a $\approx 6\,\%$ edge at the upper end of the noise floor), the best headline-window hit rates (CSI-M $0.2656$, HSS-M $0.3128$, both above the diffusion baseline), the lowest FAR-M ($0.2563$), and the best \texttt{u10} and \texttt{t2m} RMSE, conceding only a noise-floor margin on \texttt{v10}/\texttt{qpepre} RMSE to FlowCast. The $K = 1$ row trades a little of this for half the cost again: its \texttt{t2m} ($0.7498$) still leads the flow heads, but its CRPS ($0.5501$, $+5.6\,\%$ over $K = 2$) reflects the under-dispersion of Section~\ref{sec:concl-limitations} --- a single average-velocity evaluation maps each noise draw almost onto the conditional mean, narrowing ensemble spread, a deficit that compounds autoregressively --- which is why $K = 2$ is the recommended operating point. The picture is therefore no longer a simple speed-for-quality trade: at a tenth of the diffusion cost the average-velocity head is the strongest all-round candidate, with the more conservative FlowCast (lowest FAR across the wider neighbourhoods, column-leading \texttt{v10}/\texttt{qpepre} RMSE) a close alternative at $\sim$$3\times$ higher cost.

\paragraph{Per-channel RMSE and CRPS.} Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-channel} and~\ref{fig:crps-channel} break the errors out by channel, and the two flow students split the wins: FlowCast keeps the lowest \texttt{v10} and \texttt{qpepre} RMSE ($1.41$, $0.96$), while MeanFlow-$K2$ takes the lowest \texttt{u10} RMSE ($1.27$) and --- decisively --- the best \texttt{t2m} on \emph{both} metrics (RMSE $0.7315$\,K, CRPS $0.4007$), beating the diffusion baseline ($0.8023$\,K / $0.4681$) by $\approx 9\,\%$/$14\,\%$. The \texttt{qpepre} and \texttt{v10} CRPS also tip to MeanFlow (best at $K1$ and $K2$ respectively, both inside the noise floor), while the diffusion baseline holds the lowest \texttt{u10} CRPS. The temperature result is the cleanest single-channel story: MeanFlow wins \texttt{t2m} outright at both $K$, evidence that FlowCast's \texttt{t2m} weakness belongs to its learned trajectory rather than the shared backbone, loss weights, or residual pipeline. The one place MeanFlow pays for a narrowed spread is the wind CRPS columns at $K = 1$, recovered at $K = 2$.

\section{Inference Cost: Wall-Clock and NFE Ablation}
\label{sec:exp-nfe}

Tables~\ref{tab:timing} and~\ref{tab:timing-h100} report the inference-timing ablation at an operationally realistic configuration --- a 50-member, 24-hour ensemble forecast on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (the deployment GPU) and on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB --- across the EDM Heun sampler, FlowCast at $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$, and MeanFlow at $K \in \{1, 2\}$. This is the cost figure that matters for deployment: an ensemble convection-permitting cycle re-run every forecast hour. Figure~\ref{fig:timing} plots both devices' wall-clock on a log axis with each head's speedup over the EDM baseline annotated.

\paragraph{Read.} The ordering tracks the per-step NFE almost exactly. FlowCast at $K = 10$ generates a full 50-member 24-hour ensemble in \SI{645}{\second} ($\approx 10.8$ min) against the diffusion sampler's \SI{1898}{\second} ($\approx 31.6$ min), a $2.9\times$ speedup; the $K = 15$/$20$ rungs scale linearly for no headline-skill gain, which is why $K = 10$ is the FlowCast operating point. MeanFlow cuts the same forecast to \SI{239}{\second} ($\approx 4.0$ min, $7.9\times$) at $K = 2$ and \SI{192}{\second} ($\approx 3.2$ min, $9.9\times$) at $K = 1$. Per evaluation the ODE heads cost $\approx \SI{0.045}{\second}$--$\SI{0.054}{\second}$, undercutting the Heun sampler's $\approx \SI{0.044}{\second}$ only marginally per evaluation but decisively in aggregate; MeanFlow's larger nominal s\,/\,NFE ($0.10$--$0.16$\,s) is an amortisation artefact (fixed per-forecast overhead divided over one or two evaluations), not a heavier network --- the backbone is the same SongUNet as FlowCast's.

\paragraph{Operational consequence.} This is the cost that motivated the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro-cost}). A 50-member, 24-hour ensemble cycle issues $50\times24$ sequence-steps per forecast; at $35$ NFE per step the diffusion sampler performs $50\times24\times35 = 42{,}000$ network evaluations per cycle, against $12{,}000$ for FlowCast-$K10$ and just $2{,}400$ for MeanFlow-$K2$ ($1{,}200$ at $K = 1$). Converting a configuration that the diffusion sampler places at half an hour of GPU time per cycle into one that completes in four minutes is what moves an ensemble convection-permitting forecast back inside an operational between-radar-volume budget. Crucially, these speedups are obtained \emph{without} a second distillation training stage: they are intrinsic to the straight-line and average-velocity models.\footnote{The timing harness counts the EDM Heun sampler at $36$ evaluations per step (two per Heun step, last corrector retained); the $35$-NFE convention used elsewhere drops the final corrector. The one-evaluation difference does not affect any conclusion.}

\paragraph{Cross-hardware.} The H100 leg (Table~\ref{tab:timing-h100}) confirms that the ranking is hardware-independent: the H100 roughly halves every wall-clock relative to the A6000 (A6000/H100 $= 1.8$--$2.1\times$ across all six configurations), but the \emph{relative} speedups over the EDM baseline are preserved and even grow slightly --- FlowCast-$K10$ goes from $2.9\times$ on the A6000 to $3.1\times$ on the H100, and MeanFlow-$K2$ from $7.9\times$ to $8.5\times$ ($K = 1$: $9.9\times \to 10.8\times$). The mechanism is that the diffusion baseline's $35$ evaluations per step keep it compute-bound on faster silicon, while the one-to-two-evaluation MeanFlow head amortises a larger fixed-overhead fraction, so each hardware generation widens rather than narrows the gap. In absolute terms the H100 brings the MeanFlow-$K2$ cost to \SI{116}{\second} ($\approx 1.9$ min) and MeanFlow-$K1$ to \SI{91}{\second} ($\approx 1.5$ min) per 50-member 24-hour forecast, against the diffusion sampler's \SI{987}{\second} ($\approx 16.4$ min).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discussion}

The headline question of the thesis is whether a single-run, teacher-free \emph{average-velocity} residual can replace the EDM diffusion residual in a regional convection-permitting forecaster without losing the features that matter for Taiwan precipitation --- and do so cheaply enough to deploy. The evidence at the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is \emph{yes}: at $K \in \{1, 2\}$ network evaluations --- roughly a tenth of the diffusion sampler's cost --- MeanFlow reaches and, at $K = 2$, modestly exceeds the diffusion baseline's deterministic and probabilistic skill, posting the best CRPS (best in the study, though within the single-snapshot noise floor) and the best categorical hit rates of any head at the headline neighbourhood and the best temperature of any head, and runs a full 50-member 24-hour ensemble forecast in four minutes against the diffusion sampler's half hour. Its only genuine concession is the narrowed ensemble spread at $K = 1$, mostly closed by the second evaluation. The FlowCast straight-line baseline charts the intermediate point on the same trajectory axis: at $K = 10$ Euler steps it matches the diffusion baseline's CRPS, beats it on wind and precipitation RMSE, and runs $\approx 3.7\times$ faster --- confirming that the straight-line path already recovers most of the diffusion skill before the average-velocity jump removes the last of the integration cost.

These conclusions are bounded by the single-snapshot budget, the single regression mean and domain, and the precipitation-encoding gotcha of Section~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} (the regression mean and residual head must share the \texttt{log1p} encoding, or the residual distribution is silently corrupted); these are taken up in full, alongside the development-time failure modes, in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}, which lays out the concrete experiments still needed to turn this provisional result into a defensible claim.
