\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the MeanFlow residual of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} against its two reference heads --- the EDM diffusion baseline it sets out to accelerate and the FlowCast straight-line flow-matching baseline it generalises. Section~\ref{sec:exp-protocol} defines the experimental protocol and the matched-sample-budget convention. Section~\ref{sec:exp-metrics} defines the evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:exp-main} reports the headline comparison on the 2022 validation year. Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} reports the NFE / wall-clock--versus--quality trade-off for both flow heads, where MeanFlow's one-to-two-evaluation operating point is the central result. Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip} drills into precipitation skill by threshold; Section~\ref{sec:exp-rollout} reports autoregressive rollout error growth. Sections~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} and~\ref{sec:exp-qpw} report the \texttt{log1p}-encoding and precipitation-weight ablations. Section~\ref{sec:exp-legacy} places the heads against a legacy old-StormCast baseline. Section~\ref{sec:exp-qual} presents qualitative case studies, Section~\ref{sec:exp-failure} catalogues failure modes, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-discussion} concludes with a discussion.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

\paragraph{Validation split.} All reported metrics are computed on the cleaned 2022 full-year RWRF validation store (8{,}759 hourly samples; Section~\ref{sec:method-data}). This contrasts with the stale \texttt{valid\_dates} shipped in \texttt{stormcast/config/inference/stormcast.yaml}, which we override explicitly in every run.

\paragraph{Matched training-sample budget.} The fair-comparison unit is \emph{training samples seen}, not optimiser steps, because the residual heads use different global batch sizes. The EDM baseline trains at batch 64, FlowCast at batch 96, and MeanFlow at batch 112, so $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples corresponds to step $31{,}250$ for EDM and step $20{,}833$ for FlowCast; we evaluate each head at the nearest saved checkpoint to this anchor (EDM \texttt{EDMPrecond.0.31000}, FlowCast \texttt{FlowCastPrecond.0.20000}, MeanFlow \texttt{MeanFlowPrecond.0.20000}). The MeanFlow checkpoint lands at $\approx 2.24\,\text{M}$ samples, $12\,\%$ above the anchor --- small against the $10$--$30\,\%$ snapshot noise below, but noted for completeness. Holding training samples fixed means any quality difference reflects the generative trajectory and not a training-compute advantage.

\paragraph{Single-snapshot noise.} Each validation point is a single snapshot. Adjacent validation logs drift by $10$--$30\,\%$, so differences below $\approx 5\,\%$ on a single metric should not be over-interpreted; we call out only gaps that are large relative to this noise floor.

\paragraph{NFE budgets and samplers.} The EDM baseline samples with its native 18-step Heun schedule (two model evaluations per step except the last, $2N - 1 = 35$ NFE per hourly step). FlowCast samples with $K$ explicit Euler steps, $K$ NFE; we report $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ in the rollout scoreboard and sweep $K$ from $1$ to $50$ in the inference-step study of Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}. MeanFlow samples with $K$ average-velocity segments (Eq.~\eqref{eq:meanflow-sampler}), also exactly $K$ NFE; its headline operating points are $K \in \{1, 2\}$, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} sweeps it over the same $1$--$50$ grid as FlowCast. The scoreboard harness loads the two flow students from their online checkpoints in the same way, so the FlowCast-versus-MeanFlow comparison is symmetric end to end (the EMA shadow is used by the in-training validation sampler; Section~\ref{sec:method-flowcast}).

\paragraph{Rollouts and ensembling.} The scoreboard metrics are computed from autoregressive rollouts driven by \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py}, which denormalises every output (undoing standardisation and the \texttt{log1p} encoding) so that \texttt{qpepre} is scored in mm\,h$^{-1}$ regardless of the on-disk encoding. CRPS uses a \textbf{ten-member} per-hour ensemble of independent noise seeds --- matching the ensemble-size bracket of operational convection-permitting ensemble systems (e.g.\ HREF at $\approx 10$ members, MOGREPS-UK at 18) --- and deterministic and categorical scores use the ensemble mean. Ten initial times are spaced evenly across the validation year, each rolled out to $+12$\,h.

\paragraph{Hardware.} Training: multi-GPU single node. Evaluation: single GPU. The ``Time/Seq.'' column in the scoreboards is the wall-clock cost of generating one rollout sequence on the evaluation GPU and is the operationally relevant cost figure.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp-metrics}

All metrics are computed on denormalised fields with physical units (K, m\,s$^{-1}$, mm\,h$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{RMSE and MAE.} For channel $k$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|\,HW}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat{X}^{(k)}_{t,i,j} - X^{(k)}_{t,i,j}\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
with MAE defined analogously.

\paragraph{CRPS.} The continuous ranked probability score evaluates the full predictive distribution of the small per-hour ensemble against the observation; lower is better. It rewards a forecast that is both sharp and calibrated, and is our primary probabilistic score.

\paragraph{Categorical precipitation scores.} For threshold $\tau$ (mm\,h$^{-1}$), classify each pixel as wet ($\geq \tau$) or dry. With hits $H$, misses $M$, false alarms $F$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CSI}(\tau) = \frac{H}{H + M + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) = \frac{F}{H + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{HSS}(\tau) = \frac{2(H\,\mathrm{CN} - M\,F)}{(H+M)(M+\mathrm{CN}) + (H+F)(F+\mathrm{CN})},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CN}$ is the correct-negative count. We report $\tau \in \{0.1, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 16.0\}$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$; ``-M'' suffixes denote the mean over thresholds and ``-P16'' the $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ heavy-rain point. CSI and HSS reward correct detection (higher is better); FAR penalises over-prediction (lower is better).

\paragraph{Fractions Skill Score.} With neighbourhood radius $n$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FSS}(\tau, n) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j} - p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2}{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j})^2 + \sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2},
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the fraction of $\tau$-exceeding pixels in an $n \times n$ box centred at $(i,j)$. FSS mitigates the double-penalty problem and is reported at the heavy-rain point $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ for $n \in \{3, 7, 15\}$.

\paragraph{Radial log-PSD.} We compute the 2D FFT of each \texttt{qpepre} field, square its magnitude, radially average to obtain $P(\kappa)$, and compare $\log P(\kappa)$ of forecast and observation. A model that blurs precipitation has a log-PSD that decays too fast at high wavenumbers.

\paragraph{Rollout metrics.} Per-channel RMSE is additionally reported as a function of lead time $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, 12\}$\,h to expose autoregressive error growth.

\paragraph{Read.} At $K = 10$ FlowCast runs in \SI{5.88}{\second} per ten-member sequence versus \SI{21.78}{\second} for the $35$-NFE EDM Heun sampler --- a \textbf{3.7$\times$ speedup} --- and at that budget it \emph{matches or beats} the diffusion baseline on most columns: CRPS is within the snapshot noise ($0.6833$ vs $0.6783$, a $0.7\,\%$ gap), and FlowCast is clearly better on \texttt{u10} RMSE ($-4\,\%$), \texttt{v10} RMSE ($-16\,\%$), \texttt{qpepre} RMSE ($-5\,\%$), and the false-alarm ratio (FAR-M $0.42$ vs $0.47$). The diffusion baseline retains an edge on the hit-based categorical score CSI-M, on the (near-degenerate; Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip}) heavy-rain FSS, and on \texttt{t2m} RMSE (diffusion \num{1.0208} vs FlowCast \num{1.1410}; the one channel where FlowCast trails, omitted from Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard} for width but shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-channel}). Pushing FlowCast to $K = 15$ buys the best CRPS ($0.6740$) and best \texttt{qpepre} RMSE ($0.8897$) in the table at \SI{8.35}{\second}, still $2.6\times$ cheaper than diffusion; beyond that, $K = 20$ shows no further gain and a FAR regression, consistent with the straight-line ODE being essentially converged by $\approx 10$--$15$ steps.

\paragraph{Read (MeanFlow rows).} MeanFlow compresses the cost axis by another large factor: \SI{2.35}{\second} at $K = 2$ --- $\approx 9\times$ cheaper than the diffusion sampler and $2.5\times$ cheaper than FlowCast's $K = 10$ --- and \SI{1.83}{\second} at $K = 1$. At $K = 2$ it posts the best \texttt{t2m} RMSE of \emph{any} head (\num{0.9999} vs the diffusion baseline's \num{1.0208}; Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-channel}) --- repairing the one channel FlowCast loses --- together with the best HSS-M in the table ($0.1852$) and a CSI-M ($0.1492$) at the diffusion baseline's level, i.e.\ it does not inherit FlowCast's conservative hit-rate deficit. Its concessions are probabilistic and wind-deterministic: the $12$-hour rollout CRPS ($0.7265$) trails the ODE heads by $\approx 6$--$7\,\%$, FAR-M at $K = 2$ ($0.52$) sits at FlowCast's $K{=}20$ level rather than its $K{=}10$ level, and \texttt{v10} RMSE ($1.79$) lands between FlowCast ($1.64$) and diffusion ($1.96$). The $K = 1$ row makes the mechanism legible: deterministic and categorical scores stay close to $K = 2$ (and FAR-M $0.3861$ is the lowest in the table --- a narrow ensemble raises few alarms), but CRPS degrades to $0.8485$, essentially the legacy baseline's level. This is the under-dispersion failure mode of Section~\ref{sec:exp-failure} compounding autoregressively: the same checkpoint's \emph{single-step} calibration is ahead of FlowCast at every step count (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}), so the rollout gap is accumulated spread deficit, not single-step quality.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The categorical picture is a coherent trade rather than a wash: FlowCast issues fewer false alarms (lower FAR) but also slightly fewer hits (lower CSI), i.e.\ it is the more \emph{conservative} precipitation forecaster, while the diffusion baseline is more aggressive in placing wet pixels and is rewarded by CSI but penalised by FAR. Notably, the FAR gap is much smaller at the ten-member standard than in small-ensemble pilots ($0.42$ vs $0.47$, where four-member runs showed $0.43$ vs $0.70$): averaging more members suppresses the diffusion head's speculative wet pixels, so part of its apparent false-alarm problem was an ensemble-size artefact. Which trade is preferable is an operational choice; the unambiguous wins for FlowCast are the wind fields, precipitation RMSE, and a $\sim$$3.7\times$ lower inference cost at statistically indistinguishable CRPS. MeanFlow occupies a third corner of the trade space: diffusion-level hit rates and the best temperature at a tenth of the diffusion cost, paid for with a single-snapshot-scale rollout-CRPS gap whose origin (narrowed ensemble spread under autoregression) is diagnosed in Sections~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} and~\ref{sec:exp-failure}; restoring rollout spread at $K \leq 2$ is listed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\paragraph{Per-channel RMSE and CRPS.} Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-channel} and~\ref{fig:crps-channel} break the deterministic and probabilistic errors out by channel. FlowCast's advantage is largest and consistent across both metrics on the meridional wind \texttt{v10} and on the precipitation channel. On \texttt{u10} the two metrics disagree --- FlowCast has the lower RMSE but the diffusion baseline the lower CRPS at $K \leq 15$ (at $K = 20$ the two are tied) --- and on \texttt{t2m} the diffusion baseline leads both flow-ODE settings, FlowCast's temperature residual being marginally noisier at this budget. MeanFlow redraws the \texttt{t2m} column: at $K = 2$ its temperature RMSE (\num{0.9999}\,K) is the best of all heads, $2\,\%$ below the diffusion baseline and $12\,\%$ below FlowCast --- evidence that FlowCast's \texttt{t2m} weakness belongs to its learned trajectory rather than to the shared backbone, loss weights, or residual pipeline, since MeanFlow shares all three.

\section{Inference Cost: NFE and Wall-Clock Trade-off}
\label{sec:exp-nfe}

The defining property of a straight-line flow is that it integrates accurately in very few steps; the defining property of an \emph{average-velocity} model is that it does not integrate at all. The three FlowCast rows of Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard} already show that quality is essentially flat across $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ while wall-clock rises linearly, so FlowCast's operational sweet spot sits at the low end of that range; the MeanFlow rows reach the table's deterministic and categorical skill class at $K \in \{1, 2\}$ (with the rollout-CRPS concession analysed above). Figure~\ref{fig:nfe} extends both sweeps over the full $K \in \{1, \ldots, 50\}$ range on the same matched checkpoints: FlowCast's precipitation CRPS saturates by $K \approx 8$--$10$, while MeanFlow starts \emph{below} FlowCast's saturated level already at $K = 1$--$2$ --- the two heads pay for quality in different currencies, integration steps versus a harder training objective, and MeanFlow's currency is the cheaper one at inference time. Wall-clock per sequence is linear in $K$ for both, so the few-step regime is simultaneously the accurate and the cheap one. Per NFE at the $K = 10$ operating point, FlowCast costs $\approx \SI{0.59}{\second}$ per network evaluation (single forward pass, ten members); because a fixed per-sequence overhead is amortised over more steps, the effective per-NFE cost falls toward $\approx \SI{0.51}{\second}$ at $K = 20$. Either way it undercuts the diffusion Heun sampler's $\approx \SI{0.62}{\second}$ per evaluation ($35$ evaluations per hourly step), so FlowCast is cheaper both because it uses fewer steps \emph{and} because each step is a single forward pass with no second-order correction.

Table~\ref{tab:nfe} lists the full FlowCast sweep; Table~\ref{tab:nfe-meanflow} repeats it for the MeanFlow head. Two systematic trends sit underneath FlowCast's CRPS saturation. First, CRPS and ensemble-mean RMSE move in \emph{opposite} directions with $K$: CRPS improves monotonically (calibration sharpens as the ODE is integrated more accurately) while \texttt{qpepre} RMSE of the ensemble mean is actually \emph{best at $K = 1$} and drifts up by $\approx 2\,\%$ at $K = 50$ --- coarse integration acts as an implicit smoother, pulling members toward a common (RMSE-friendly, structure-poor) field. Second, FAR-M rises steadily with $K$ ($\approx 0.11$--$0.12$ at $K \leq 3$ to $\approx 0.25$ at $K = 50$): better-integrated members place more, sharper wet pixels and incur more false alarms, converging toward the diffusion baseline's behaviour. Both trends reinforce the operational conclusion already drawn from Figure~\ref{fig:nfe}: past $K \approx 10$ the only things still changing are wall-clock and the conservative-to-aggressive trade-off, not headline skill.

Three readings of Table~\ref{tab:nfe-meanflow} are specific to the average-velocity objective. The recommended operating point therefore stays at $K = 2$. (The isolated FAR/CSI dip at $K = 4$ is a single-configuration outlier of this small sweep subset and is not interpreted.)

The practical consequence is the one that motivated the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro-cost}). A 50-member, 24-hour ensemble rollout costs, per forecast cycle, $50\times24$ sequence-steps; at $\approx \SI{0.59}{\second}$ per NFE and $K = 10$, FlowCast brings the generative head's contribution down by the same $\approx 3.7\times$ factor seen in the single-sequence timing, moving a configuration that the $35$-NFE diffusion sampler places outside the between-radar-volume budget back inside it. MeanFlow at $K = 2$ divides the generative evaluation count by a further $5\times$: the same 50-member cycle needs $50\times24\times2 = 2{,}400$ network evaluations against $42{,}000$ for the diffusion sampler, cheap enough that the ensemble size itself --- not the sampler --- becomes the budget knob. Crucially, these speedups are obtained \emph{without} a second distillation training stage: they are intrinsic to the straight-line and average-velocity models.

\section{Precipitation Skill by Threshold}
\label{sec:exp-precip}

Mean categorical scores hide the threshold dependence that matters operationally. Figure~\ref{fig:csi-threshold} plots CSI, FAR, and HSS against the rainfall threshold, and Figure~\ref{fig:fss} the heavy-rain FSS at three neighbourhood sizes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/csi_per_threshold.png}
  \caption[Categorical precipitation skill by threshold]{CSI, FAR, and HSS as a function of rainfall threshold for the diffusion baseline, FlowCast at $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$, and MeanFlow at $K \in \{1, 2\}$ (ten-member ensemble means). The flow heads hold a FAR advantage at every threshold; the diffusion baseline pulls ahead on CSI at the heavier $5$--$10$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ thresholds.}
  \label{fig:csi-threshold}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Light to moderate rain.} At $\tau = 0.1$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ the two heads are close on CSI (diffusion $0.509$, FlowCast-$K20$ $0.524$, FlowCast-$K10$ $0.507$), but FlowCast's false-alarm ratio is substantially lower ($0.33$ vs $0.46$). The same ordering holds at $\tau = 1.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ (CSI: diffusion $0.303$ vs FlowCast-$K15$ $0.290$; FAR: FlowCast-$K10$ $0.39$ vs diffusion $0.62$). This is the quantitative form of the ``more conservative'' behaviour noted above: FlowCast does not paint speculative light rain across dry pixels.

\paragraph{Heavy rain.} At $\tau = 5.0$ and $10.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ the diffusion baseline leads on CSI ($0.075$ vs $0.040$ at $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$), reflecting its more aggressive placement of intense cells. FlowCast's heavy-rain hit rate at this $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is its weakest point and a clear target for the longer-training and tail-loss experiments listed in the project to-do. At the heavy-rain point itself the comparison degenerates: with ten-member ensemble \emph{means}, almost no pixel of either head exceeds $16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, so FSS-P16 is zero for FlowCast and $\leq 0.002$ for diffusion at every neighbourhood size (Figure~\ref{fig:fss}). This is an ensemble-mean artefact --- averaging members suppresses extremes --- rather than a statement about individual members, and it is precisely why per-member (probabilistic) heavy-rain scoring over larger ensembles is listed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/results/fss_p16.png}
  \caption[Heavy-rain Fractions Skill Score]{FSS at $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ for neighbourhood sizes $n \in \{3, 7, 15\}$, computed on ten-member ensemble means, for all three heads. The score is near-zero across the board: averaging ten members suppresses $\geq 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ extremes almost entirely, so this threshold mainly diagnoses ensemble-mean smoothing rather than model skill. Heavy-rain assessment at the member level requires probabilistic scoring (future work).}
  \label{fig:fss}
\end{figure}

\section{Autoregressive Rollout}
\label{sec:exp-rollout}

Single-step skill understates operational risk because errors compound under autoregression. Figure~\ref{fig:rollout} plots per-channel RMSE against lead time out to 12 hours.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/rollout_rmse.png}
  \caption[Rollout RMSE versus lead time]{Per-channel RMSE as a function of autoregressive lead time (1--12\,h) for the legacy baseline, the EDM diffusion baseline, FlowCast, and MeanFlow at $K \in \{1, 2\}$. FlowCast tracks or improves on the diffusion baseline for the winds and precipitation across the horizon; MeanFlow at $K{=}2$ holds the lowest \texttt{t2m} error of the cleaned heads, and neither flow head destabilises under rollout.}
  \label{fig:rollout}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Read.} Both heads show the expected monotone error growth. For \texttt{v10} FlowCast stays below the diffusion baseline at every lead time (e.g.\ \SI{1.98}{\metre\per\second} vs \SI{2.58}{\metre\per\second} at 10\,h); for \texttt{u10} the two are close, with FlowCast slightly ahead at long lead times (\SI{1.67}{\metre\per\second} vs \SI{1.94}{\metre\per\second} at 10\,h). The \texttt{qpepre} rollout RMSE is comparable between heads and does \emph{not} diverge --- the conservative FlowCast precipitation does not destabilise under rollout. The one adverse signal is \texttt{t2m}: FlowCast's temperature error grows faster after $\approx 8$\,h (reaching $\approx \SI{2.0}{\kelvin}$ at 10--12\,h versus $\approx \SI{1.7}{\kelvin}$ for diffusion), consistent with its weaker single-step \texttt{t2m} number. No run exhibited the unphysical blow-up that aggressive few-step diffusion samplers are prone to, which we attribute to the straight-line ODE's stability.

\section{Ablation: \texttt{log1p} Precipitation Encoding}
\label{sec:exp-log1p}

The single most consequential preprocessing decision is whether to store \texttt{qpepre} as raw mm\,h$^{-1}$ or as $\log(1+x)$ before standardisation. Table~\ref{tab:log1p} reports the within-architecture A/B test (single-step standardised validation RMSE at $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples) for the EDM and FlowCast heads. MeanFlow is absent from this table by necessity: only a log1p MeanFlow was trained, so its raw-mm/h cell does not exist. Because the mechanism identified below is head-agnostic --- the badly-scaled raw channel contaminates the shared U-Net trunk --- and MeanFlow shares that trunk, loss weighting, and the FlowCast objective on $75\,\%$ of its batch, we adopt log1p for MeanFlow without a separate ablation.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption[log1p versus raw-mm/h encoding ablation]{Single-step standardised validation RMSE at $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples, log1p versus raw-mm/h qpepre encoding. Values are dimensionless standard scores. \textbf{Bold} = better within each architecture pair on the non-qpepre channels. The qpepre column is \emph{not} directly comparable across the encoding boundary (different units in standardised space); see the note.}
  \label{tab:log1p}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
    \toprule
    Run & RMSE t2m & RMSE u10 & RMSE v10 & RMSE qpepre$^\dagger$ \\
    \midrule
    diffusion, log1p    & \textbf{0.0994} & \textbf{0.3523} & \textbf{0.2762} & 0.7865 \\
    diffusion, raw mm/h & 0.1245 & 0.3955 & 0.3115 & 0.4156 \\
    FlowCast, log1p     & \textbf{0.0835} & \textbf{0.2931} & 0.2463 & 0.3851 \\
    FlowCast, raw mm/h  & 0.1118 & 0.3079 & \textbf{0.2331} & 0.3471 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Read.} The \texttt{log1p} encoding improves \emph{both} heads on \texttt{t2m} and \texttt{u10} ($\sim$$20$--$25\,\%$ on \texttt{t2m}, $\sim$$5$--$11\,\%$ on \texttt{u10}) and improves the diffusion baseline's \texttt{v10} as well; the lone exception is FlowCast's \texttt{v10}, where the two encodings differ by less than the $10$--$30\,\%$ snapshot noise and so are effectively tied. The mechanism is that the badly-scaled raw \texttt{qpepre} channel (standard deviation $9.12$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$) propagates through the shared U-Net trunk and degrades the other channels; compressing it to a standard deviation of $\approx 0.37$ in log space removes that pressure. The net benefit is similar for both architectures, so the encoding choice is essentially independent of the head, and we adopt \texttt{log1p} as canonical throughout. The qpepre column ($\dagger$) cannot be compared directly across the encoding boundary: in standardised space the two encodings carry different units, and inverting \texttt{log1p} on a scalar RMSE is invalid because \texttt{expm1} does not commute with the root-mean-square. The mm\,h$^{-1}$-correct comparison is the denormalised scoreboard of Section~\ref{sec:exp-main}, where \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py} inverts the encoding per pixel before scoring.

\section{Ablation: Precipitation Channel Weight}
\label{sec:exp-qpw}

The precipitation weight $w_{\text{pr}}$ (the last entry of \texttt{channel\_weights}) trades precipitation skill against wind/temperature skill. Table~\ref{tab:qpw} reports the full eight-point FlowCast sweep $w_{\text{pr}} \in [1.0, 2.4]$ (single-step standardised validation RMSE, log1p, $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples). The sweep is FlowCast-only: $w_{\text{pr}}$ is a training-time parameter, so a MeanFlow sweep would require seven additional training runs. \section{Failure Modes}
\label{sec:exp-failure}

We document four failure modes observed during development.

\paragraph{Heavy-rain under-detection.} The flow heads' weakest metric is pixel-exact CSI at the heavier thresholds ($5$--$10$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$): their conservative, low-false-alarm behaviour costs hits on intense cells. Diagnosis: the per-threshold CSI curve (Figure~\ref{fig:csi-threshold}) crossing below the diffusion baseline above $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, partly recovered by the neighbourhood FSS. Mitigation candidates (deferred to future work): longer training, a higher $w_{\text{pr}}$ paired with the spectral term, or an explicit tail-quantile loss on \texttt{qpepre}.

\paragraph{\texttt{t2m} rollout drift.} FlowCast's temperature error grows faster than the diffusion baseline's after $\approx 8$ rollout hours (Figure~\ref{fig:rollout}). This is the autoregressive signature of its weaker single-step \texttt{t2m} number. Notably, MeanFlow does \emph{not} inherit this drift despite sharing FlowCast's backbone and loss weights --- its \texttt{t2m} rollout error stays at or below the diffusion baseline's --- which suggests the drift is a property of the learned trajectory rather than of the architecture. Mitigation for FlowCast: a per-channel weight that does not starve \texttt{t2m}, or a short teacher-forcing warmup.

\paragraph{One-evaluation under-dispersion (MeanFlow).} At $K = 1$ MeanFlow's deterministic skill is intact but its CRPS lags its own $K = 2$ setting consistently: a single average-velocity evaluation maps each noise draw almost straight onto the conditional mean, narrowing ensemble spread and costing calibration. One extra segment ($K = 2$) restores most of the spread at one additional NFE, which is why $K = 2$ is the recommended MeanFlow operating point and the validation default. The effect \emph{compounds under autoregression}: in the single-step sweep the $K{=}1$-to-$K{=}2$ CRPS gap is $6\,\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:nfe-meanflow}), but over $12$-hour rollouts it widens to $17\,\%$ ($0.8485$ vs $0.7265$, Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard}) because each hour's narrowed ensemble seeds the next; the residual $\approx 6\,\%$ rollout-CRPS gap that remains at $K = 2$ against the ODE heads has the same origin, even though MeanFlow's \emph{single-step} calibration is the best in the study (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}). Diagnosis: the CRPS columns of Tables~\ref{tab:nfe-meanflow} and~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard}; per-member rank histograms and rollout spread restoration are future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\paragraph{EMA dependence.} Loading the online \texttt{checkpoint\_*.pt} training-state dump instead of the proper weights produces visibly noisier flow-head samples. This is not a model failure but an evaluation pitfall. The in-training validation samplers use the EMA shadow (\texttt{ema\_state.pt}); the head-to-head harness of this chapter loads the online \texttt{.mdlus} checkpoints for FlowCast and MeanFlow \emph{symmetrically}, so the A/B comparison between the two students is unaffected by the convention, and the EMA-versus-online gap itself shrinks as training proceeds.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discussion}

The headline question of the thesis is whether a single-run, teacher-free \emph{average-velocity} residual can replace the EDM diffusion residual in a regional convection-permitting forecaster without losing the features that matter for Taiwan precipitation --- and do so cheaply enough to deploy. The evidence at the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is a qualified \emph{yes}: at $K \in \{1, 2\}$ network evaluations --- roughly a tenth of the diffusion sampler's cost --- MeanFlow reaches the diffusion baseline's deterministic and categorical skill class, posts the best \texttt{t2m} accuracy of any head, and delivers a single-step precipitation CRPS below the FlowCast baseline at any step count, conceding only a spread-driven rollout-CRPS gap (largest at $K = 1$, mostly closed by $K = 2$). The FlowCast straight-line baseline charts the intermediate point on the same trajectory axis: at $K = 10$ Euler steps it matches the diffusion baseline's CRPS, beats it on wind and precipitation RMSE, cuts its false-alarm ratio by $\approx 11\,\%$ (FAR-M $0.42$ vs $0.47$), and runs $\approx 3.7\times$ faster --- confirming that the straight-line path already recovers most of the diffusion skill before the average-velocity jump removes the last of the integration cost.

Three caveats bound these conclusions. First, all numbers are at a single $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample snapshot with $10$--$30\,\%$ validation noise; the FlowCast canonical run continues to $\approx 13.4\,\text{M}$ samples (step $140{,}000$) and a final comparison at converged budgets is part of the remaining work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} and the project to-do). Second, both heads are anchored to the same frozen regression mean and the same cleaned log1p dataset, so the comparison is clean but specific to this Taiwan RWRF setup; transfer to other domains is untested. Third, the precipitation-encoding gotcha of Section~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} means the regression mean and residual head must always share the \texttt{log1p} encoding --- mixing encodings silently corrupts the residual distribution. The next chapter summarises what we have learned and lays out the concrete experiments and data still needed to turn this provisional result into a defensible thesis claim.
