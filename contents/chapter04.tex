\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter presents the empirical comparison between the FlowCast residual of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} and the EDM diffusion baseline. \section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

\paragraph{Validation split.} All reported metrics are computed on the cleaned 2022 full-year RWRF validation store (8{,}759 hourly samples; Section~\ref{sec:method-data}). This contrasts with the stale \texttt{valid\_dates} shipped in \texttt{stormcast/config/inference/stormcast.yaml}, which we override explicitly in every run.

\paragraph{Matched training-sample budget.} The fair-comparison unit is \emph{training samples seen}, not optimiser steps, because the two residual heads use different global batch sizes. The EDM baseline trains at batch 64 and FlowCast at batch 96, so $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples corresponds to step $31{,}250$ for EDM and step $20{,}833$ for FlowCast. We evaluate each head at the nearest saved checkpoint to this $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample anchor (EDM \texttt{EDMPrecond.0.31000}, FlowCast \texttt{FlowCastPrecond.0.20000} with its EMA shadow). Holding training samples fixed means any quality difference reflects the generative trajectory and not a training-compute advantage.

\paragraph{Single-snapshot noise.} Each validation point is a single snapshot. Adjacent validation logs drift by $10$--$30\,\%$, so differences below $\approx 5\,\%$ on a single metric should not be over-interpreted; we call out only gaps that are large relative to this noise floor.

\paragraph{NFE budgets and samplers.} The EDM baseline samples with its native Heun schedule ($\approx 25$ NFE). FlowCast samples with $K$ explicit Euler steps, $K$ NFE; we report $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ in the rollout scoreboard and sweep down to $K = 1$ in the inference-step study of Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}. FlowCast always uses its EMA shadow weights, never the online checkpoint.

\paragraph{Rollouts and ensembling.} The scoreboard metrics are computed from autoregressive rollouts driven by \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py}, which denormalises every output (undoing standardisation and the \texttt{log1p} encoding) so that \texttt{qpepre} is scored in mm\,h$^{-1}$ regardless of the on-disk encoding. CRPS uses a small per-hour ensemble of independent noise seeds; deterministic scores use the ensemble mean.

\paragraph{Hardware.} Training: multi-GPU single node. Evaluation: single GPU. The ``Time/Seq.'' column in the scoreboards is the wall-clock cost of generating one rollout sequence on the evaluation GPU and is the operationally relevant cost figure.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp-metrics}

All metrics are computed on denormalised fields with physical units (K, m\,s$^{-1}$, mm\,h$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{RMSE and MAE.} For channel $k$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|\,HW}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat{X}^{(k)}_{t,i,j} - X^{(k)}_{t,i,j}\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
with MAE defined analogously.

\paragraph{CRPS.} The continuous ranked probability score evaluates the full predictive distribution of the small per-hour ensemble against the observation; lower is better. It rewards a forecast that is both sharp and calibrated, and is our primary probabilistic score.

\paragraph{Categorical precipitation scores.} For threshold $\tau$ (mm\,h$^{-1}$), classify each pixel as wet ($\geq \tau$) or dry. With hits $H$, misses $M$, false alarms $F$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CSI}(\tau) = \frac{H}{H + M + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) = \frac{F}{H + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{HSS}(\tau) = \frac{2(H\,\mathrm{CN} - M\,F)}{(H+M)(M+\mathrm{CN}) + (H+F)(F+\mathrm{CN})},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CN}$ is the correct-negative count. We report $\tau \in \{0.1, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 16.0\}$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$; ``-M'' suffixes denote the mean over thresholds and ``-P16'' the $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ heavy-rain point. CSI and HSS reward correct detection (higher is better); FAR penalises over-prediction (lower is better).

\paragraph{Fractions Skill Score.} With neighbourhood radius $n$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FSS}(\tau, n) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j} - p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2}{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j})^2 + \sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2},
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the fraction of $\tau$-exceeding pixels in an $n \times n$ box centred at $(i,j)$. FSS mitigates the double-penalty problem and is reported at the heavy-rain point $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ for $n \in \{3, 7, 15\}$.

\paragraph{Radial log-PSD.} We compute the 2D FFT of each \texttt{qpepre} field, square its magnitude, radially average to obtain $P(\kappa)$, and compare $\log P(\kappa)$ of forecast and observation. A model that blurs precipitation has a log-PSD that decays too fast at high wavenumbers.

\paragraph{Rollout metrics.} Per-channel RMSE is additionally reported as a function of lead time $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, 12\}$\,h to expose autoregressive error growth.

Which is preferable is an operational choice; the unambiguous wins for FlowCast are the wind fields, precipitation RMSE, calibrated CRPS, and a $\sim$$3\times$ lower inference cost.

FlowCast's advantage is largest and consistent across both metrics on the meridional wind \texttt{v10} and on the precipitation channel. On \texttt{u10} the two metrics disagree --- FlowCast has the lower RMSE but the diffusion baseline the lower CRPS --- and on \texttt{t2m} the diffusion baseline leads on both, FlowCast's temperature residual being marginally noisier at this budget.

\section{Inference Cost: NFE and Wall-Clock Trade-off}
\label{sec:exp-nfe}

The defining property of a straight-line flow is that it integrates accurately in very few steps. The three FlowCast rows of Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard} already show that quality is essentially flat across $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ while wall-clock rises linearly, so the operational sweet spot sits at the low end of that range. Figure~\ref{fig:nfe} extends the sweep over the full $K \in \{1, \ldots, 50\}$ range on the same matched checkpoint: precipitation CRPS saturates by $K \approx 8$--$10$, and the wall-clock per sequence is linear in $K$, so the few-step regime is simultaneously the accurate and the cheap one. Per NFE at the $K = 10$ operating point, FlowCast costs $\approx \SI{0.84}{\second}$ per network evaluation (single forward pass); because a fixed per-sequence overhead is amortised over more steps, the effective per-NFE cost actually falls toward $\approx \SI{0.69}{\second}$ at $K = 20$. Either way it undercuts the diffusion Heun sampler's $\approx \SI{0.98}{\second}$ per evaluation (two evaluations per interior step), so FlowCast is cheaper both because it uses fewer steps \emph{and} because each step is a single forward pass with no second-order correction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/results/nfe_pareto.png}
  \caption[FlowCast NFE Pareto sweep]{FlowCast quality and cost as the Euler step count $K$ is swept, holding the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample checkpoint fixed (2022 validation, single generative step). (a)~precipitation CRPS is essentially converged by $K \approx 8$--$10$; the shaded band marks the $K \in \{10, 20\}$ range tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard}, which sits on the flat part of the curve. (b)~wall-clock per sequence grows linearly in $K$ (dotted reference anchored at the most-amortised point), so the few-step regime is also the cheap regime. The head-to-head against the diffusion baseline is in Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard}; this sweep is FlowCast-only and on a smaller sequence subset, so absolute magnitudes are not directly comparable to the scoreboard.}
  \label{fig:nfe}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:nfe} lists the full sweep. Two systematic trends sit underneath the CRPS saturation. First, CRPS and ensemble-mean RMSE move in \emph{opposite} directions with $K$: CRPS improves monotonically (calibration sharpens as the ODE is integrated more accurately) while \texttt{qpepre} RMSE of the ensemble mean is actually \emph{best at $K = 1$} and drifts up by $\approx 6\,\%$ at $K = 50$ --- coarse integration acts as an implicit smoother, pulling members toward a common (RMSE-friendly, structure-poor) field. Second, FAR-M rises steadily with $K$ ($0.11$ at $K \leq 3$ to $0.35$ at $K = 50$): better-integrated members place more, sharper wet pixels and incur more false alarms, converging toward the diffusion baseline's behaviour. Both trends reinforce the operational conclusion already drawn from Figure~\ref{fig:nfe}: past $K \approx 10$ the only things still changing are wall-clock and the conservative-to-aggressive trade-off, not headline skill.

The practical consequence is the one that motivated the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro-cost}). A 50-member, 24-hour ensemble rollout costs, per forecast cycle, $50\times24$ sequence-steps; at $\approx \SI{0.84}{\second}$ per NFE and $K = 10$, FlowCast brings the generative head's contribution down by the same $\approx 3\times$ factor seen in the single-sequence timing, moving a configuration that the $25$-NFE diffusion sampler places outside the between-radar-volume budget back inside it. Crucially, this speedup is obtained \emph{without} a second distillation training stage: it is intrinsic to the straight-line model.

\section{Precipitation Skill by Threshold}
\label{sec:exp-precip}

Mean categorical scores hide the threshold dependence that matters operationally. Figure~\ref{fig:csi-threshold} plots CSI, FAR, and HSS against the rainfall threshold, and Figure~\ref{fig:fss} the heavy-rain FSS at three neighbourhood sizes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/csi_per_threshold.png}
  \caption[Categorical precipitation skill by threshold]{CSI, FAR, and HSS as a function of rainfall threshold for the diffusion baseline and FlowCast at $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$. FlowCast holds a clear FAR advantage at every threshold; the diffusion baseline pulls ahead on CSI at the heavier $5$--$10$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ thresholds.}
  \label{fig:csi-threshold}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Light to moderate rain.} At $\tau = 0.1$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ the two heads are close on CSI (diffusion $0.505$, FlowCast-$K20$ $0.512$, FlowCast-$K10$ $0.481$), but FlowCast's false-alarm ratio is substantially lower ($0.34$ vs $0.45$). The same ordering holds at $\tau = 1.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$. This is the quantitative form of the ``more conservative'' behaviour noted above: FlowCast does not paint speculative light rain across dry pixels.

\paragraph{Heavy rain.} At $\tau = 5.0$ and $10.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ the diffusion baseline leads on CSI ($0.082$ vs $0.046$ at $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$), reflecting its more aggressive placement of intense cells. FlowCast's heavy-rain hit rate at this $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is its weakest point and a clear target for the longer-training and tail-loss experiments listed in the project to-do. Interestingly, FlowCast's neighbourhood FSS at $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:fss}) is \emph{higher} than the diffusion baseline's at $K = 10$: when a near-miss is scored against a spatial neighbourhood rather than pixel-exactly, FlowCast's heavy-rain structure is competitive even though its pixel-exact CSI is lower --- evidence that the gap is partly a displacement/double-penalty artefact rather than a pure miss.

\section{Autoregressive Rollout}
\label{sec:exp-rollout}

Single-step skill understates operational risk because errors compound under autoregression. Figure~\ref{fig:rollout} plots per-channel RMSE against lead time out to 12 hours.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/rollout_rmse.png}
  \caption[Rollout RMSE versus lead time]{Per-channel RMSE as a function of autoregressive lead time (1--12\,h) for the legacy baseline, the EDM diffusion baseline, and FlowCast. FlowCast tracks or improves on the diffusion baseline for the winds and precipitation across the horizon; its \texttt{t2m} error grows somewhat faster at long lead times.}
  \label{fig:rollout}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Read.} Both heads show the expected monotone error growth. For \texttt{v10} FlowCast stays below the diffusion baseline at every lead time (e.g.\ \SI{2.02}{\metre\per\second} vs \SI{2.74}{\metre\per\second} at 10\,h); for \texttt{u10} the two are close, with FlowCast slightly ahead. The \texttt{qpepre} rollout RMSE is comparable between heads and does \emph{not} diverge --- the conservative FlowCast precipitation does not destabilise under rollout. The one adverse signal is \texttt{t2m}: FlowCast's temperature error grows faster after $\approx 8$\,h (reaching $\approx \SI{2.0}{\kelvin}$ at 10--12\,h versus $\approx \SI{1.7}{\kelvin}$ for diffusion), consistent with its weaker single-step \texttt{t2m} number. No run exhibited the unphysical blow-up that aggressive few-step diffusion samplers are prone to, which we attribute to the straight-line ODE's stability.

\section{Ablation: \texttt{log1p} Precipitation Encoding}
\label{sec:exp-log1p}

The single most consequential preprocessing decision is whether to store \texttt{qpepre} as raw mm\,h$^{-1}$ or as $\log(1+x)$ before standardisation. Table~\ref{tab:log1p} reports the within-architecture A/B test (single-step standardised validation RMSE at $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples) for both heads.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption[log1p versus raw-mm/h encoding ablation]{Single-step standardised validation RMSE at $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples, log1p versus raw-mm/h qpepre encoding. Values are dimensionless standard scores. \textbf{Bold} = better within each architecture pair on the non-qpepre channels. The qpepre column is \emph{not} directly comparable across the encoding boundary (different units in standardised space); see the note.}
  \label{tab:log1p}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
    \toprule
    Run & RMSE t2m & RMSE u10 & RMSE v10 & RMSE qpepre$^\dagger$ \\
    \midrule
    diffusion, log1p    & \textbf{0.0994} & \textbf{0.3523} & \textbf{0.2762} & 0.7865 \\
    diffusion, raw mm/h & 0.1245 & 0.3955 & 0.3115 & 0.4156 \\
    FlowCast, log1p     & \textbf{0.0835} & \textbf{0.2931} & 0.2463 & 0.3851 \\
    FlowCast, raw mm/h  & 0.1118 & 0.3079 & \textbf{0.2331} & 0.3471 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Read.} The \texttt{log1p} encoding improves \emph{both} heads on \texttt{t2m} and \texttt{u10} ($\sim$$20$--$25\,\%$ on \texttt{t2m}, $\sim$$5$--$11\,\%$ on \texttt{u10}) and improves the diffusion baseline's \texttt{v10} as well; the lone exception is FlowCast's \texttt{v10}, where the two encodings differ by less than the $10$--$30\,\%$ snapshot noise and so are effectively tied. The mechanism is that the badly-scaled raw \texttt{qpepre} channel (standard deviation $9.12$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$) propagates through the shared U-Net trunk and degrades the other channels; compressing it to a standard deviation of $\approx 0.37$ in log space removes that pressure. The net benefit is similar for both architectures, so the encoding choice is essentially independent of the head, and we adopt \texttt{log1p} as canonical throughout. The qpepre column ($\dagger$) cannot be compared directly across the encoding boundary: in standardised space the two encodings carry different units, and inverting \texttt{log1p} on a scalar RMSE is invalid because \texttt{expm1} does not commute with the root-mean-square. The mm\,h$^{-1}$-correct comparison is the denormalised scoreboard of Section~\ref{sec:exp-main}, where \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py} inverts the encoding per pixel before scoring.

\paragraph{Read.} There is \emph{no clean monotone trend}: both $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.4$ and $w_{\text{pr}} = 2.0$ beat their neighbours, while $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.6$ and $w_{\text{pr}} \geq 2.2$ are poor across the board. $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.4$ is the per-channel sweet spot for the \emph{wind} fields (lowest \texttt{u10} and \texttt{v10} RMSE in the sweep), whereas $w_{\text{pr}} = 2.0$ is best on \texttt{t2m}; on \texttt{qpepre} the minimum is a near-tie among $w_{\text{pr}} \in \{1.2, 1.4, 2.0\}$ that sits inside the $10$--$30\,\%$ single-snapshot noise, so we do not read fine ordering between those three. The robust conclusions are (i) some up-weighting of \texttt{qpepre} helps but the floor-to-ceiling spread is large and real (e.g.\ $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.4$ at \num{0.3996} vs $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.6$ at \num{0.6796} is a $\sim$$70\,\%$ jump on \texttt{qpepre} RMSE); (ii) $w_{\text{pr}} \approx 2.0$ is a reasonable all-round default that wins \texttt{t2m} and is competitive on \texttt{qpepre}, which is why the canonical run adopts it; and (iii) the wind/precipitation trade-off is genuine and would justify dropping to $w_{\text{pr}} = 1.4$ if the winds were the priority.

\section{Failure Modes}
\label{sec:exp-failure}

We document three failure modes observed during development.

\paragraph{Heavy-rain under-detection.} FlowCast's weakest metric is pixel-exact CSI at the heavier thresholds ($5$--$10$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$): its conservative, low-false-alarm behaviour costs hits on intense cells. Diagnosis: the per-threshold CSI curve (Figure~\ref{fig:csi-threshold}) crossing below the diffusion baseline above $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, partly recovered by the neighbourhood FSS. Mitigation candidates (deferred to future work): longer training, a higher $w_{\text{pr}}$ paired with the spectral term, or an explicit tail-quantile loss on \texttt{qpepre}.

\paragraph{\texttt{t2m} rollout drift.} FlowCast's temperature error grows faster than the diffusion baseline's after $\approx 8$ rollout hours (Figure~\ref{fig:rollout}). This is the autoregressive signature of its weaker single-step \texttt{t2m} number. Mitigation: a per-channel weight that does not starve \texttt{t2m}, or a short teacher-forcing warmup.

\paragraph{EMA dependence.} Loading the online \texttt{checkpoint\_*.pt} instead of \texttt{ema\_state.pt} produces visibly noisier FlowCast samples. This is not a model failure but an evaluation pitfall; all reported FlowCast numbers use the EMA shadow, and the evaluation scripts default to it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discussion}

The headline question of the thesis is whether a single-run, teacher-free flow-matching residual can replace the EDM diffusion residual in a regional convection-permitting forecaster without losing the features that matter for Taiwan precipitation --- and do so cheaply enough to deploy. The evidence at the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is a qualified \emph{yes}: at $K = 10$ Euler steps FlowCast matches the diffusion baseline's CRPS, beats it on wind and precipitation RMSE, cuts its false-alarm ratio by $\approx 38\,\%$ (FAR-M $0.43$ vs $0.70$), and runs $\approx 2.9\times$ faster, while trading away some pixel-exact heavy-rain hit rate and some \texttt{t2m} accuracy.

Three caveats bound these conclusions. First, all numbers are at a single $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample snapshot with $10$--$30\,\%$ validation noise; the FlowCast canonical run continues to $\approx 13.4\,\text{M}$ samples (step $140{,}000$) and a final comparison at converged budgets is part of the remaining work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} and the project to-do). Second, both heads are anchored to the same frozen regression mean and the same cleaned log1p dataset, so the comparison is clean but specific to this Taiwan RWRF setup; transfer to other domains is untested. Third, the precipitation-encoding gotcha of Section~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} means the regression mean and residual head must always share the \texttt{log1p} encoding --- mixing encodings silently corrupts the residual distribution. The next chapter summarises what we have learned and lays out the concrete experiments and data still needed to turn this provisional result into a defensible thesis claim.
