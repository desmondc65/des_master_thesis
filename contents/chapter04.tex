\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the MeanFlow residual of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} against its two reference heads --- the EDM diffusion baseline it sets out to accelerate and the FlowCast straight-line flow-matching baseline it generalises. Section~\ref{sec:exp-protocol} defines the experimental protocol and the matched-sample-budget convention. Section~\ref{sec:exp-metrics} defines the evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:exp-main} reports the headline comparison on the 2022 validation year. Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} reports the inference-cost (wall-clock and NFE) ablation, where MeanFlow's one-to-two-evaluation operating point is the central result. Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip} drills into precipitation skill by threshold and neighbourhood size; Section~\ref{sec:exp-rollout} reports autoregressive rollout error growth. Sections~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} and~\ref{sec:exp-qpw} report the \texttt{log1p}-encoding and precipitation-weight ablations. Section~\ref{sec:exp-legacy} places the heads against a legacy old-StormCast baseline. Section~\ref{sec:exp-qual} presents qualitative case studies, Section~\ref{sec:exp-failure} catalogues failure modes, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-discussion} concludes with a discussion.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

\paragraph{Validation split.} All reported metrics are computed on the cleaned 2022 full-year RWRF validation store (8{,}759 hourly samples; Section~\ref{sec:method-data}). This contrasts with the stale \texttt{valid\_dates} shipped in \texttt{stormcast/config/inference/stormcast.yaml}, which we override explicitly in every run.

\paragraph{Matched training-sample budget.} The fair-comparison unit is \emph{training samples seen}, not optimiser steps, because the residual heads use different global batch sizes. The EDM baseline trains at batch 64, FlowCast at batch 96, and MeanFlow at batch 112, so $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples corresponds to step $31{,}250$ for EDM and step $20{,}833$ for FlowCast; we evaluate each head at the nearest saved checkpoint to this anchor (EDM \texttt{EDMPrecond.0.31000}, FlowCast \texttt{FlowCastPrecond.0.20000}, MeanFlow \texttt{MeanFlowPrecond.0.18000}). The MeanFlow checkpoint lands at $\approx 2.0\,\text{M}$ samples (batch $112 \times 18{,}000$), essentially on the anchor. Holding training samples fixed means any quality difference reflects the generative trajectory and not a training-compute advantage.

\paragraph{Single-snapshot noise.} Each validation point is a single snapshot. Adjacent validation logs drift by $10$--$30\,\%$, so differences below $\approx 5\,\%$ on a single metric should not be over-interpreted; we call out only gaps that are large relative to this noise floor.

\paragraph{NFE budgets and samplers.} The EDM baseline samples with its native 18-step Heun schedule (two model evaluations per step except the last, $2N - 1 = 35$ NFE per hourly step). FlowCast samples with $K$ explicit Euler steps, $K$ NFE; we use $K = 10$ in the headline scoreboard, the point at which the straight-line ODE is essentially converged (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}), and report the $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ cost ladder in the timing ablation. MeanFlow samples with $K$ average-velocity segments (Eq.~\eqref{eq:meanflow-sampler}), also exactly $K$ NFE; its headline operating points are $K \in \{1, 2\}$. The scoreboard harness loads the two flow students from their online checkpoints in the same way, so the FlowCast-versus-MeanFlow comparison is symmetric end to end (the EMA shadow is used by the in-training validation sampler; Section~\ref{sec:method-flowcast}).

\paragraph{Rollouts and ensembling.} The scoreboard metrics are computed from autoregressive rollouts driven by \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py}, which denormalises every output (undoing standardisation and the \texttt{log1p} encoding) so that \texttt{qpepre} is scored in mm\,h$^{-1}$ regardless of the on-disk encoding. Following the StormCast ensemble design \citep{pathak2024stormcast}, CRPS uses a \textbf{five-member} per-hour ensemble of independent noise seeds propagated autoregressively. Deterministic and categorical scores use the ensemble mean. Ten initial times are spaced evenly across the validation year, each rolled out autoregressively over a \textbf{six-hour} horizon ($+1\,\text{h}\,\ldots\,+6\,\text{h}$), matching StormCast's headline 1--6\,h skill window.

\paragraph{Hardware.} Training: multi-GPU single node. Evaluation: single GPU. The ``Time/Seq.'' column in the scoreboards is the wall-clock cost of generating one five-member six-hour rollout sequence on the evaluation GPU; the dedicated inference-timing ablation of Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe} reports the operationally relevant cost at a 50-member, 24-hour configuration on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp-metrics}

All metrics are computed on denormalised fields with physical units (K, m\,s$^{-1}$, mm\,h$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{RMSE and MAE.} For channel $k$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|\,HW}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat{M}^{(k)}_{t,i,j} - M^{(k)}_{t,i,j}\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
with MAE defined analogously.

\paragraph{CRPS.} The continuous ranked probability score evaluates the full predictive distribution of the small per-hour ensemble against the observation; lower is better. It rewards a forecast that is both sharp and calibrated, and is our primary probabilistic score.

\paragraph{Categorical precipitation scores.} For threshold $\tau$ (mm\,h$^{-1}$), classify each pixel as wet ($\geq \tau$) or dry. With hits $H$, misses $M$, false alarms $F$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CSI}(\tau) = \frac{H}{H + M + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) = \frac{F}{H + F}, \quad
  \mathrm{HSS}(\tau) = \frac{2(H\,\mathrm{CN} - M\,F)}{(H+M)(M+\mathrm{CN}) + (H+F)(F+\mathrm{CN})},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CN}$ is the correct-negative count. We report $\tau \in \{0.1, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 16.0\}$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$; ``-M'' suffixes denote the mean over thresholds and ``-P16'' the $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ heavy-rain point. CSI and HSS reward correct detection (higher is better); FAR penalises over-prediction (lower is better). To relax the single-pixel double-penalty, CSI/POD/FAR/HSS are scored with \emph{neighbourhood-max} pooling: an event counts as forecast if any pixel within an $n \times n$ window exceeds $\tau$. We evaluate at the four StormCast pooling windows \citep{pathak2024stormcast} on the $\approx 2$\,km RWRF grid --- $3$\,km (grid scale, $1\times1$\,px), $15$\,km ($7\times7$\,px), $27$\,km ($13\times13$\,px) and $45$\,km ($23\times23$\,px) --- and take the $15$\,km window as the headline.

\paragraph{Fractions Skill Score.} With neighbourhood window $n$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FSS}(\tau, n) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j} - p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2}{\sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{fc}}_{i,j})^2 + \sum_{i,j}(p^{\text{ob}}_{i,j})^2},
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the \emph{fraction} of $\tau$-exceeding pixels in the $n \times n$ box centred at $(i,j)$ \citep{roberts2008fss}. FSS mitigates the double-penalty problem and is reported at the same four pooling windows; the heavy-rain point $\tau = 16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ is denoted FSS-P16.

\paragraph{Radial log-PSD.} We compute the 2D FFT of each \texttt{qpepre} field, square its magnitude, radially average to obtain $P(\kappa)$, and compare $\log P(\kappa)$ of forecast and observation. A model that blurs precipitation has a log-PSD that decays too fast at high wavenumbers.

\paragraph{Rollout metrics.} Per-channel RMSE is additionally reported as a function of lead time $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}$\,h to expose autoregressive error growth.

\paragraph{Read.} At $K = 10$ FlowCast runs in \SI{3.07}{\second} per five-member sequence versus \SI{11.23}{\second} for the $35$-NFE EDM Heun sampler --- a \textbf{3.7$\times$ speedup} --- and at that budget it \emph{matches or beats} the diffusion baseline on most columns. CRPS is in fact marginally better ($0.5518$ vs $0.5537$, a $0.3\,\%$ gap, i.e.\ statistically indistinguishable), and FlowCast is clearly ahead on \texttt{u10} RMSE ($-5\,\%$), \texttt{v10} RMSE ($-12\,\%$), and \texttt{qpepre} RMSE ($-7\,\%$). The diffusion baseline retains an edge on the hit-based categorical scores at the headline window --- CSI-M ($0.2622$ vs $0.2504$) and HSS-M ($0.2926$ vs $0.2889$) --- and on \texttt{t2m} RMSE (diffusion \num{0.8023} vs FlowCast \num{0.8498}; the one channel where FlowCast trails, omitted from Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard} for width but shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-channel}). The two FAR figures are within noise at $15$\,km ($0.3504$ vs $0.3417$), but FlowCast is the lower-false-alarm head at the $3$, $27$ and $45$\,km windows (Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip}). Pushing FlowCast beyond $K = 10$ buys no headline skill, only wall-clock (Section~\ref{sec:exp-nfe}), consistent with the straight-line ODE being essentially converged by $\approx 10$ steps.

\paragraph{Read (MeanFlow rows).} MeanFlow compresses the cost axis by another large factor: \SI{1.10}{\second} at $K = 2$ --- $\approx 10.2\times$ cheaper than the diffusion sampler and $2.8\times$ cheaper than FlowCast's $K = 10$ --- and \SI{0.91}{\second} at $K = 1$. The notable result is that, at the matched budget, this speed comes with no quality penalty at $K = 2$: it posts the \emph{best CRPS in the table} ($0.5207$, ahead of FlowCast's $0.5518$ and the diffusion baseline's $0.5537$ --- a $\approx 6\,\%$ edge, at the upper end of the single-snapshot noise floor but consistent across the per-channel breakdown below), the best categorical hit rates at the headline window (CSI-M $0.2656$ and HSS-M $0.3128$, both \emph{above} the diffusion baseline's $0.2622$/$0.2926$; $K = 1$ is marginally higher still at $0.2689$/$0.3131$), and the \emph{lowest} FAR-M in the table ($0.2563$): the head that places the most wet pixels also raises the fewest false alarms at this neighbourhood. On the deterministic side it takes the best \texttt{u10} RMSE ($1.2651$) and the best \texttt{t2m} RMSE ($0.7315$, omitted from the table for width; Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-channel}), with \texttt{v10} ($1.4286$) and \texttt{qpepre} ($0.9637$) within $\approx 1$--$2\,\%$ of FlowCast's column-leading values. In short, at two network evaluations MeanFlow matches or beats every head on all but the \texttt{v10} and \texttt{qpepre} RMSE channels, where FlowCast holds a noise-floor margin. The $K = 1$ row trades a little of this for half the cost again: deterministic \texttt{t2m} ($0.7498$, still the best of the flow heads and below the diffusion baseline's $0.8023$) sits alongside the weakest CRPS of the cleaned heads ($0.5501$, which nonetheless still essentially matches FlowCast's $0.5518$) and the weakest wind CRPS columns. The CRPS difference from $K = 2$ to $K = 1$ ($0.5207 \to 0.5501$, $+5.6\,\%$) is the under-dispersion failure mode of Section~\ref{sec:exp-failure}: a single average-velocity evaluation maps each noise draw almost onto the conditional mean, narrowing ensemble spread, and the deficit compounds autoregressively. One extra segment restores most of the spread, which is why $K = 2$ is the recommended MeanFlow operating point.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The picture is no longer a simple speed-for-quality trade. FlowCast remains the most \emph{conservative} precipitation forecaster --- the lowest false-alarm ratio across the \emph{wider} neighbourhoods --- and holds the column-leading \texttt{v10} and \texttt{qpepre} RMSE and a $\sim$$3.7\times$ speedup at CRPS parity with the diffusion baseline. MeanFlow at $K = 2$, however, no longer occupies a quality-conceding corner: at a \emph{tenth} of the diffusion cost it posts the best CRPS of any head (by a modest $\approx 6\,\%$ margin), the best categorical hit rates \emph{and} the lowest false-alarm ratio at the headline window, and the best \texttt{u10} and \texttt{t2m} RMSE, conceding only a noise-floor margin on \texttt{v10}/\texttt{qpepre} RMSE to FlowCast. Its one genuine cost appears at $K = 1$, where a narrowed ensemble spread under autoregression lifts the rollout CRPS (diagnosed in Section~\ref{sec:exp-failure}); restoring that spread at $K = 1$ is listed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}. The operational reading is that, at this matched budget, the average-velocity head is the strongest all-round candidate, with the straight-line FlowCast head a close, slightly more precipitation-RMSE-accurate alternative at $\sim$$3\times$ higher cost.

\paragraph{Per-channel RMSE and CRPS.} Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-channel} and~\ref{fig:crps-channel} break the deterministic and probabilistic errors out by channel, and the two flow students split the wins. FlowCast keeps the lowest \texttt{v10} and \texttt{qpepre} RMSE ($1.41$ and $0.96$), while MeanFlow-$K2$ takes the lowest \texttt{u10} RMSE overall ($1.27$) and --- decisively --- the best \texttt{t2m} on \emph{both} metrics (RMSE $0.7315$\,K, CRPS $0.4007$), beating the diffusion baseline ($0.8023$\,K / $0.4681$) by $\approx 9\,\%$ and $\approx 14\,\%$. The probabilistic precipitation and meridional-wind channels also tip toward MeanFlow: \texttt{qpepre} CRPS is best at MeanFlow-$K1$ ($0.2772$, just under FlowCast's $0.2814$) and \texttt{v10} CRPS is best at MeanFlow-$K2$ ($0.7395$ vs FlowCast's $0.7420$) --- both inside the noise floor, but no longer FlowCast wins. On \texttt{u10} the diffusion baseline holds the lowest CRPS ($0.659$, with MeanFlow-$K2$ $0.662$ and FlowCast $0.667$ close behind). The temperature result is the cleanest single-channel story: MeanFlow wins \texttt{t2m} outright on both metrics at both $K$ values, evidence that FlowCast's \texttt{t2m} weakness (the noisiest temperature residual at this budget) belongs to its learned trajectory rather than to the shared backbone, loss weights, or residual pipeline, since MeanFlow shares all three. The one place MeanFlow pays for a narrowed spread is the \emph{wind} CRPS columns at $K = 1$ (\texttt{u10} CRPS $0.709$, the weakest of the cleaned heads), recovered at $K = 2$.

\section{Inference Cost: Wall-Clock and NFE Ablation}
\label{sec:exp-nfe}

Tables~\ref{tab:timing} and~\ref{tab:timing-h100} report the inference-timing ablation at an operationally realistic configuration --- a 50-member, 24-hour ensemble forecast on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (the deployment GPU) and on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB --- across the EDM Heun sampler, FlowCast at $K \in \{10, 15, 20\}$, and MeanFlow at $K \in \{1, 2\}$. This is the cost figure that matters for deployment: an ensemble convection-permitting cycle re-run every forecast hour. Figure~\ref{fig:timing} plots both devices' wall-clock on a log axis with each head's speedup over the EDM baseline annotated.

\paragraph{Read.} The ordering tracks the per-step NFE almost exactly, because wall-clock is dominated by network evaluations. FlowCast at $K = 10$ generates a full 50-member 24-hour ensemble in \SI{645}{\second} ($\approx 10.8$ min) against the diffusion sampler's \SI{1898}{\second} ($\approx 31.6$ min), a $2.9\times$ speedup; the $K = 15$ and $K = 20$ rungs scale the cost linearly ($826$\,s, $1086$\,s) for no headline-skill gain (Section~\ref{sec:exp-main}), which is why $K = 10$ is the FlowCast operating point. MeanFlow at $K = 2$ cuts the same ensemble forecast to \SI{239}{\second} ($\approx 4.0$ min, $7.9\times$ over diffusion and $2.7\times$ over FlowCast-$K10$), and at $K = 1$ to \SI{192}{\second} ($\approx 3.2$ min, $9.9\times$). Per network evaluation, the ODE heads cost $\approx \SI{0.045}{\second}$--$\SI{0.054}{\second}$, undercutting the diffusion Heun sampler's $\approx \SI{0.044}{\second}$ only marginally on a per-evaluation basis but decisively in aggregate because they need so many fewer evaluations and each is a single forward pass with no second-order corrector. MeanFlow's larger nominal s\,/\,NFE ($0.10$--$0.16$\,s) is an amortisation artefact: with only one or two evaluations, the fixed per-forecast overhead (data movement, denormalisation, metric I/O) is divided over a tiny NFE count rather than reflecting a more expensive forward pass --- the network itself is the same SongUNet as FlowCast's.

\paragraph{Operational consequence.} This is the cost that motivated the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro-cost}). A 50-member, 24-hour ensemble cycle issues $50\times24$ sequence-steps per forecast; at $35$ NFE per step the diffusion sampler performs $50\times24\times35 = 42{,}000$ network evaluations per cycle, against $12{,}000$ for FlowCast-$K10$ and just $2{,}400$ for MeanFlow-$K2$ ($1{,}200$ at $K = 1$). Converting a configuration that the diffusion sampler places at half an hour of GPU time per cycle into one that completes in four minutes is what moves an ensemble convection-permitting forecast back inside an operational between-radar-volume budget. Crucially, these speedups are obtained \emph{without} a second distillation training stage: they are intrinsic to the straight-line and average-velocity models.\footnote{The timing harness counts the EDM Heun sampler at $36$ evaluations per step (two per Heun step, last corrector retained); the $35$-NFE convention used elsewhere drops the final corrector. The one-evaluation difference does not affect any conclusion.}

\paragraph{Cross-hardware.} The H100 leg (Table~\ref{tab:timing-h100}) confirms that the ranking is hardware-independent: the H100 roughly halves every wall-clock relative to the A6000 (A6000/H100 $= 1.8$--$2.1\times$ across all six configurations), but the \emph{relative} speedups over the EDM baseline are preserved and even grow slightly --- FlowCast-$K10$ goes from $2.9\times$ on the A6000 to $3.1\times$ on the H100, and MeanFlow-$K2$ from $7.9\times$ to $8.5\times$ ($K = 1$: $9.9\times \to 10.8\times$). The mechanism is that the diffusion baseline's $35$ evaluations per step keep it compute-bound on faster silicon, while the one-to-two-evaluation MeanFlow head amortises a larger fixed-overhead fraction, so each hardware generation widens rather than narrows the gap. In absolute terms the H100 brings the MeanFlow-$K2$ cost to \SI{116}{\second} ($\approx 1.9$ min) and MeanFlow-$K1$ to \SI{91}{\second} ($\approx 1.5$ min) per 50-member 24-hour forecast, against the diffusion sampler's \SI{987}{\second} ($\approx 16.4$ min).

\section{Precipitation Skill by Threshold and Neighbourhood}

\paragraph{Light to moderate rain.} At $\tau = 0.1$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ ($15$\,km) the heads are close on CSI (diffusion $0.650$, FlowCast $0.660$, MeanFlow-$K2$ $0.680$), but the flow heads' false-alarm ratios are markedly lower than the diffusion baseline's (FlowCast $0.204$ and MeanFlow-$K2$ $0.188$ vs diffusion $0.290$). The same ordering holds at $\tau = 1.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, where the flow heads cluster near FAR $0.32$ (FlowCast $0.322$, MeanFlow-$K2$ $0.320$) against the diffusion baseline's $0.429$. This is the quantitative form of the ``more conservative'' behaviour of the flow heads: they do not paint speculative light rain across the dry pixels the way the diffusion baseline does, with MeanFlow-$K2$ now the lowest-false-alarm head at the headline window.

\paragraph{Heavy rain.} At $\tau = 5.0$ and $10.0$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ the heads converge to low CSI ($\approx 0.13$--$0.17$ at $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, $15$\,km, with MeanFlow-$K2$ marginally ahead), reflecting how hard intense convective cells are to place exactly at the $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget. Heavy-rain skill is the clearest target for the longer-training and tail-loss experiments listed in the project to-do. At the $16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ point the comparison degenerates: with five-member ensemble \emph{means}, almost no pixel of any head exceeds $16$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, so FSS-P16 is at or below $0.03$ for all heads at every neighbourhood size (Figure~\ref{fig:fss}; MeanFlow-$K2$ is the relative best of the cleaned heads at FSS-P16 $\le 0.030$, still negligible). This is an ensemble-mean artefact --- averaging members suppresses extremes --- rather than a statement about individual members, and it is precisely why per-member (probabilistic) heavy-rain scoring over larger ensembles is listed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\section{Ablation: \texttt{log1p} Precipitation Encoding}
\label{sec:exp-log1p}

The single most consequential preprocessing decision is whether to store \texttt{qpepre} as raw mm\,h$^{-1}$ or as $\log(1+x)$ before standardisation. Table~\ref{tab:log1p} reports the within-architecture A/B test (single-step standardised validation RMSE at $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples) for the EDM and FlowCast heads. MeanFlow is absent from this table by necessity: only a log1p MeanFlow was trained, so its raw-mm/h cell does not exist. Because the mechanism identified below is head-agnostic --- the badly-scaled raw channel contaminates the shared U-Net trunk --- and MeanFlow shares that trunk, loss weighting, and the FlowCast objective on $75\,\%$ of its batch, we adopt log1p for MeanFlow without a separate ablation.

The qpepre column is \emph{not} directly comparable across the encoding boundary (different units in standardised space); see the note.}

    diffusion, raw mm/h & 0.1245 & 0.3955 & 0.3115 & 0.4156 \\

\paragraph{Read.} The \texttt{log1p} encoding improves \emph{both} heads on \texttt{t2m} and \texttt{u10} ($\sim$$20$--$25\,\%$ on \texttt{t2m}, $\sim$$5$--$11\,\%$ on \texttt{u10}) and improves the diffusion baseline's \texttt{v10} as well; the lone exception is FlowCast's \texttt{v10}, where the two encodings differ by less than the $10$--$30\,\%$ snapshot noise and so are effectively tied. The mechanism is that the badly-scaled raw \texttt{qpepre} channel (standard deviation $9.12$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$) propagates through the shared U-Net trunk and degrades the other channels; compressing it to a standard deviation of $\approx 0.37$ in log space removes that pressure. The net benefit is similar for both architectures, so the encoding choice is essentially independent of the head, and we adopt \texttt{log1p} as canonical throughout. The qpepre column ($\dagger$) cannot be compared directly across the encoding boundary: in standardised space the two encodings carry different units, and inverting \texttt{log1p} on a scalar RMSE is invalid because \texttt{expm1} does not commute with the root-mean-square. The mm\,h$^{-1}$-correct comparison is the denormalised scoreboard of Section~\ref{sec:exp-main}, where \texttt{compare\_diffusion\_vs\_flowcast.py} inverts the encoding per pixel before scoring.

\section{Ablation: Precipitation Channel Weight}
\label{sec:exp-qpw}

The precipitation weight $w_{\text{pr}}$ (the last entry of \texttt{channel\_weights}) trades precipitation skill against wind/temperature skill. Table~\ref{tab:qpw} reports the full eight-point FlowCast sweep $w_{\text{pr}} \in [1.0, 2.4]$ (single-step standardised validation RMSE, log1p, $\approx 2\,\text{M}$ samples). \section{Qualitative Case Studies}
\label{sec:exp-qual}

To complement the aggregate metrics we examine the single most precipitation-active initial time of the 2022 validation year --- $t_0 = $ 2022-10-15 21:00\,UTC, a heavy convective episode with a peak RWRF rain rate of $\approx 80$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ and $\approx 48\,\%$ of the domain wet at $\geq 1$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ --- and roll every head forward autoregressively over the same $+1$\,h\,$\ldots$\,$+6$\,h horizon used for the rollout metrics. Figure~\ref{fig:qual-qpepre} stacks the precipitation fields (RWRF truth, legacy EDM, cleaned EDM diffusion, FlowCast, MeanFlow), and Figure~\ref{fig:qual-u10} the zonal wind, all denormalised to physical units; the model rows load the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample checkpoints used throughout the chapter.

\paragraph{Precipitation.} The case renders the categorical trade-offs of Section~\ref{sec:exp-precip} visible. The cleaned EDM diffusion head paints the broadest, most diffuse precipitation --- widespread moderate rain that bleeds into nominally dry pixels, the spatial signature of its higher false-alarm ratio at the fine windows. FlowCast is the most conservative head: it keeps the dry regions dry but visibly \emph{damps} the most intense convective cores, and the heavy-rain signal washes out as the rollout advances --- the qualitative form of its low FAR bought at the cost of heavy-rain hits. MeanFlow at two evaluations occupies the middle ground the metrics predict: it retains markedly more high-intensity structure than FlowCast, re-forming bright $\geq 20$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ cells late in the rollout (consistent with its best CSI-M/HSS-M hit rates), while still suppressing the diffusion baseline's speculative light rain. Crucially MeanFlow's two-evaluation fields are not visibly smoother than FlowCast's ten-step fields --- the average-velocity sampler does not pay the blurring penalty an aggressively truncated ODE integration would --- and none of the cleaned flow heads destabilises over the six hours (no unphysical excursions), echoing the bounded rollout RMSE of Table~\ref{tab:rollout-rmse}. The legacy EDM panel sits on the wider $224{\times}128$ grid (a different field of view) and is visibly grainier.

\paragraph{Wind.} On \texttt{u10} (Figure~\ref{fig:qual-u10}) all heads track the broad-scale flow closely --- the wind channels are far easier than precipitation --- with the cleaned flow heads' fields lying closest to the RWRF target, consistent with their lower wind RMSE (Section~\ref{sec:exp-main}) and the absence of wind-field drift under rollout.

\section{Failure Modes}
\label{sec:exp-failure}

We document four failure modes observed during development.

\paragraph{Heavy-rain under-detection.} The flow heads' weakest metric is pixel-exact CSI at the heavier thresholds ($5$--$10$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$): their conservative, low-false-alarm behaviour costs hits on intense cells, and at the $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget all heads converge to low heavy-rain CSI. Diagnosis: the per-threshold CSI curve (Figure~\ref{fig:csi-threshold}) flattening toward zero above $5$\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, partly recovered by the wider-neighbourhood FSS. Mitigation candidates (deferred to future work): longer training, a higher $w_{\text{pr}}$, or an explicit tail-quantile loss on \texttt{qpepre}.

\paragraph{\texttt{t2m} rollout drift.} FlowCast's temperature error grows fastest of the cleaned heads under rollout, reaching \SI{1.73}{\kelvin} at $+6$\,h against the diffusion baseline's \SI{1.60}{\kelvin} (Figure~\ref{fig:rollout}). This is the autoregressive signature of its weaker single-step \texttt{t2m} number. Notably, MeanFlow does \emph{not} inherit this drift despite sharing FlowCast's backbone and loss weights --- its \texttt{t2m} rollout error stays \emph{below} the diffusion baseline's level (\SI{1.57}{\kelvin} at both $K = 1$ and $K = 2$) --- which suggests the drift is a property of the learned trajectory rather than of the architecture. Mitigation for FlowCast: a per-channel weight that does not starve \texttt{t2m}, or a short teacher-forcing warmup.

\paragraph{One-evaluation under-dispersion (MeanFlow).} At $K = 1$ MeanFlow's deterministic skill is intact (its \texttt{t2m} is among the best of any head) but its CRPS lags its own $K = 2$ setting: a single average-velocity evaluation maps each noise draw almost straight onto the conditional mean, narrowing ensemble spread and costing calibration. The effect compounds under autoregression because each hour's narrowed ensemble seeds the next, so the rollout CRPS gap is the visible symptom: $0.5501$ at $K = 1$ versus $0.5207$ at $K = 2$ (a $5.6\,\%$ deficit; Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard}), with the wind CRPS columns of Figure~\ref{fig:crps-channel} showing the same signature. One extra segment ($K = 2$) restores most of the spread at one additional NFE, which is why $K = 2$ is the recommended MeanFlow operating point and the validation default. Diagnosis: the CRPS columns of Table~\ref{tab:main-scoreboard} and Figure~\ref{fig:per-channel}; per-member rank histograms and rollout spread restoration are future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\paragraph{EMA dependence.} Loading the online \texttt{checkpoint\_*.pt} training-state dump instead of the proper weights produces visibly noisier flow-head samples. This is not a model failure but an evaluation pitfall. The in-training validation samplers use the EMA shadow (\texttt{ema\_state.pt}); the head-to-head harness of this chapter loads the online \texttt{.mdlus} checkpoints for FlowCast and MeanFlow \emph{symmetrically}, so the A/B comparison between the two students is unaffected by the convention, and the EMA-versus-online gap itself shrinks as training proceeds.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discussion}

The headline question of the thesis is whether a single-run, teacher-free \emph{average-velocity} residual can replace the EDM diffusion residual in a regional convection-permitting forecaster without losing the features that matter for Taiwan precipitation --- and do so cheaply enough to deploy. The evidence at the matched $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample budget is \emph{yes}: at $K \in \{1, 2\}$ network evaluations --- roughly a tenth of the diffusion sampler's cost --- MeanFlow reaches and, at $K = 2$, modestly exceeds the diffusion baseline's deterministic and probabilistic skill, posting the best CRPS and the best categorical hit rates of any head at the headline neighbourhood and the best temperature of any head, and runs a full 50-member 24-hour ensemble forecast in four minutes against the diffusion sampler's half hour. Its only genuine concession is the narrowed ensemble spread at $K = 1$, mostly closed by the second evaluation. The FlowCast straight-line baseline charts the intermediate point on the same trajectory axis: at $K = 10$ Euler steps it matches the diffusion baseline's CRPS, beats it on wind and precipitation RMSE, and runs $\approx 3.7\times$ faster --- confirming that the straight-line path already recovers most of the diffusion skill before the average-velocity jump removes the last of the integration cost.

Three caveats bound these conclusions. First, all numbers are at a single $\approx 2\,\text{M}$-sample snapshot with $10$--$30\,\%$ validation noise; the FlowCast canonical run continues to $\approx 13.4\,\text{M}$ samples (step $140{,}000$) and a final comparison at converged budgets is part of the remaining work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} and the project to-do). Second, both heads are anchored to the same frozen regression mean and the same cleaned log1p dataset, so the comparison is clean but specific to this Taiwan RWRF setup; transfer to other domains is untested. Third, the precipitation-encoding gotcha of Section~\ref{sec:exp-log1p} means the regression mean and residual head must always share the \texttt{log1p} encoding --- mixing encodings silently corrupts the residual distribution. The next chapter summarises what we have learned and lays out the concrete experiments and data still needed to turn this provisional result into a defensible thesis claim.
